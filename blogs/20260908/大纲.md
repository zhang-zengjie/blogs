# *Why Formalizing Regulations Is So Difficult?*

**Target length:** ~2,500–3,300 words
**Reading time:** ~10–15 min

## 1. Why Must Machines Follow Rules?

**~300–400 words | 10–15%**

### 1.1 From human-readable regulations to machine-interpretable rules

* ADS/ADAS does not operate outside the regulatory environment.
* If compliance is to be assessed computationally, natural-language regulations eventually need to be represented in a form that machines can interpret, monitor, test, or verify.
* Introduce the basic transition:

> Regulation → Formalization → Machine-checkable specification

### 1.2 The apparent problem: syntax

* At first sight, formalization appears to be mainly a problem of translating natural-language statements into logical structures:

  * conditions
  * predicates
  * temporal relations
  * thresholds
  * constraints
* This part is difficult, but **not the deepest difficulty**.

### 1.3 The hidden problem: semantics

Use **“Maintain a safe distance.”** as the anchor.

Briefly introduce TTC/RSS as examples of seemingly precise ways to operationalize safety.

Then expose the semantic questions:

* Which speed and distance should be used?
* How should reaction time be determined?
* What threshold makes a TTC “safe”?
* When is TTC itself meaningful?
* When does a numerical violation actually correspond to a violation of the regulation?

### 1.4 Chapter takeaway

Establish the central distinction that governs the rest of the article:

> **Formalizing a regulation has two very different layers: syntax and semantics.**

**Syntax can often be designed.
Semantics must remain connected to the real world.**

The apparent surface problem is syntax; the much larger hidden problem is semantics.

---

# 2. What Exactly Are the Challenges?

**~600–700 words | ~20%**

**Scope restriction:** From this point onward, discuss **semantic formalization**, not generic “natural language → logic” translation.

## 2.1 Underspecified semantics: where should the boundary be?

Examples:

* safe distance
* reasonable care
* appropriate speed
* as soon as practicable

Key point:

* Regulations need to balance **coverage and flexibility**.
* Therefore, legal language may deliberately leave semantic space for contextual interpretation.
* A syntactically precise formalization can therefore still encode different semantic assumptions.
* Different formalizations may look structurally equivalent while imposing substantially different behavioral boundaries.

**Core point:**

> The ambiguity is not necessarily a defect in the syntax of the regulation; it can be an intentional property of its semantics.

---

## 2.2 Syntax can be designed; semantics are contextual

Start from the simplified logical form:

> **IF A holds given context C and the anticipated behavior of others, then B should hold in context D.**

Contrast:

* syntax: conditions, operators, temporal relations, logical structure
* semantics: what A, B, C and D actually mean in the physical and social world

The central question:

> **Where does the missing context come from?**

Discuss briefly:

* road environment
* vehicle state
* other road users
* uncertainty
* human behavior and intention
* interaction between agents

The point is not that LTL/STL or other formalisms are incapable of representing context.

The point is:

> **A formally precise expression is only as meaningful as the semantic assumptions used to construct it.**

---

## 2.3 Rules are coupled through shared context

Move beyond ambiguity of individual rules.

Rules depend on:

* exceptions
* precedence
* other regulations
* road conditions
* interactions between road users
* shared variables describing the same traffic situation

Frame this as **contextual coupling**:

> Different rules may depend on the same entities and the same contextual variables.

Therefore, the challenge is not merely:

> translating sentences into formulas

but:

> **reconstructing an interpretation system with ambiguous decision thresholds, uncertain conditions, and shared contextual variables.**

This is the final synthesis of Chapter 2.

### Chapter takeaway

The problem has now changed:

> We are no longer asking *“How do we encode this sentence?”*

but:

> **“What semantic commitment are we making when we encode it?”**

---

# 3. What Are We Actually Formalizing?

**~550–650 words | ~20%**

This is the **first major conceptual lift**.

## 3.1 Regulation ≠ Specification

Start with:

> **When we formalize a regulation, are we actually formalizing the regulation itself?**

Answer:

> **Not quite.**

A formalization is an **interpretation of the regulation**:

> a particular interpretation
> under a particular operational context
> for a particular system.

Establish:

* regulations are written primarily for human interpretation;
* formal specifications are constructed for machine reasoning;
* these are different representational regimes;
* therefore formalization is not a neutral translation process.

### Key point

**Formalization is an act of interpretation.**

It creates a computational representation of what the regulation is taken to mean under specified assumptions.

---

## 3.2 Interpretation for whom?

Introduce the question without going too far into legal theory:

> **Who is the intended consumer of this interpretation?**

Potential purposes:

* communication among humans
* defining expected behavior of an ADS
* system development
* verification and validation
* regulatory assessment
* certification / homologation
* post-incident analysis

The same regulation may therefore admit different levels of operationalization depending on **what the interpretation is intended to accomplish**.

The important point is not to resolve this question, but to establish:

> **The purpose of interpretation influences how much semantic space needs to be compressed.**

---

## 3.3 Who is authorized to make that interpretation?

This is the key turn.

Ask:

> **Who is authorized to decide where the semantic boundary lies?**

This moves the problem beyond technical formalization.

The question is no longer simply:

> Can researchers construct a sufficiently precise formula?

but:

> **Who has the authority to determine that this formula is an acceptable interpretation of the regulation?**

Establish the boundary:

* researcher can design a formalization;
* engineer can operationalize a behavior;
* verifier can establish whether the system satisfies the specification;
* but **the legitimacy of the semantic interpretation is not purely a technical decision.**

### Chapter takeaway

The bottleneck therefore moves:

> **from formal-language design → to authorized semantic interpretation.**

The unresolved challenge is how an authorized agency can further compress the semantic space of mature regulations sufficiently for human–machine compatibility without arbitrarily redefining the regulation itself.


# 4. What Is Formal Verification Really For?

**~900–1,000 words | ~30%**

**This is the main meta-lift of the article.**

The purpose of this section is **not to argue for or against formal verification**, but to determine its **feasible boundary**.

The key idea is:

> Before asking whether an ADS can be formally verified, we need to understand what must already be determined before verification can meaningfully begin.

The capability boundary of formal verification emerges from four progressively narrower questions: **behavioral objectives → contextual interpretation → syntactic formalization → system verification.**

---

## 4.1 Four boundaries that determine the verification objective

Rather than treating “verification” as one monolithic problem, decompose the verification objective into four layers. Each layer removes a different source of uncertainty and establishes a boundary for what can subsequently be verified.

All four should be anchored in **existing engineering and regulatory paradigms**, rather than presented as abstract philosophical categories.

### A. What should an ADS **not** do?

**Normative / safety-boundary question**

The first and often more tractable question is not:

> *What should an ADS do?*

but:

> **What behavior should an ADS never be allowed to exhibit?**

This connects directly to established safety paradigms:

* **ISO 26262** — hazards arising from malfunctioning behavior / functional safety
* **ISO 21448 (SOTIF)** — risks arising from insufficient specification or insufficient performance of the intended functionality
* **Responsibility-sensitive approaches such as RSS** — explicit constraints on what constitutes irresponsible or unsafe behavior

The important point is that formal verification does not need to determine the entire normative objective of driving.

It can operate on a much narrower question:

> Given a defined hazard, responsibility rule, or safety property, what behavior must be excluded?

This already establishes the first boundary:

> **The verification target must be defined before it can be verified.**

---

### B. What does the rule mean **within a defined operational context**?

**Interpretation / context question**

A regulation does not operate in a vacuum.

Existing ADS safety practice already introduces an important boundary through the **Operational Design Domain (ODD)**:

* where the system operates
* under what environmental conditions
* under what road conditions
* under what operational constraints

The ODD therefore provides a first explicit boundary for interpretation.

But an important gap remains:

> **The ODD largely defines the operating environment of the ADS, but not the complete semantic state of a multi-agent traffic interaction.**

A traffic situation is not only:

> *Where am I? What is the weather? What is the road condition?*

It may also involve:

* what other road users are doing;
* what they are likely to do next;
* how their behavior interacts with the ADS;
* who has priority or responsibility;
* how one agent's action changes the feasible actions of others.

This is where the interpretation problem becomes fundamentally **multi-agent**.

Existing frameworks such as SOTIF, scenario-based safety assessment, and responsibility models already provide parts of this structure, but they do not collapse the entire semantic problem into one universally accepted interpretation model.

The boundary is therefore:

> **The verification problem begins only after the relevant context—and the assumptions about other agents within that context—have been sufficiently specified.**

---

### C. How far can regulation be translated **syntactically**?

**Formalization / syntax question**

Once the semantic interpretation and context have been fixed, a different question emerges:

> **Can that interpretation be translated into a formal language without introducing unresolved semantic assumptions?**

This is deliberately a narrower question than “Can regulations be formalized?”

Here, temporarily **hold context fixed**.

The problem becomes one of representation:

* predicates
* logical operators
* temporal relations
* numerical thresholds
* state transitions
* constraints
* preconditions and postconditions

The relevant research tradition already demonstrates that substantial portions of traffic rules and safety requirements **can** be represented formally.

Examples include:

* temporal logic specifications;
* rule-based representations;
* formalized traffic rules;
* machine-checkable constraints;
* formal safety contracts.

But the important boundary is not whether formal syntax is expressive enough in principle.

It is:

> **Which semantic commitments have already been fixed before the syntax is constructed?**

A syntactically correct translation does not recover semantic information that was never specified.

Thus:

> **Syntax can be formalized to the extent that its underlying semantic interpretation has already been fixed.**

This temporarily allows the article to “push context outside the box” and ask a much cleaner question:

> Given a fixed interpretation, how faithfully and completely can it be represented formally?

---

### D. Given the specification, how can we establish that the system satisfies it?

**Verification / evidence question**

Only now does the classical verification problem appear:

> Given a formal specification, assumptions, system model, and implementation, does the system satisfy the specified property?

Here, two complementary routes emerge.

**Formal verification**

* model checking
* theorem proving
* formal contracts
* reachability / invariant reasoning
* runtime verification

These can provide strong guarantees **within the defined model and assumptions**.

But when a complete proof of correctness-by-construction is impractical, infeasible, or too restrictive, verification can move toward:

**Scenario-based testing and validation**

supported by established frameworks such as the **ISO 3450x series**, where scenario definition, scenario selection, test objectives, and systematic testing provide an evidence-based alternative/complement to exhaustive formal proof.

The important distinction is therefore not:

> formal verification **versus** testing

but:

> **What level of assurance can be established for a given specification, model, and scenario space?**

This establishes the final boundary:

> **Verification can establish properties of a defined system under defined assumptions; it cannot retroactively determine whether those assumptions were the correct interpretation of the regulation.**

---

## 4.2 The boundary of formal verification

The four boundaries above collectively define the feasible scope of formal verification.

Insert the conceptual figure here:

> **REGULATION**
> ↓
> **Interpretation Layer**
> → Responsibility Model
> → Safety Model
> → Ethical & Rights Model
> → Traffic Performance Model
> ↓
> **Formalizable Core**
> ↓
> **Formal Verification / Scenario-based Validation**
> ↓
> **ADS / System Behavior**

The key visual distinction:

**Inside the interpretation boundary**

* explicit assumptions
* defined context
* defined semantic commitments
* formal properties
* mathematical models
* formal verification
* potentially automated V-type iteration

Here, the verification loop can become increasingly rigorous and increasingly automated.

**Outside the interpretation boundary**

* contextual meaning
* normative judgment
* ambiguity
* multi-agent interaction assumptions
* interpretation of human-oriented rules
* selection of relevant evidence

These remain areas where **human expertise and institutional coordination** are required.

This is the central conclusion of the chapter:

> **Formal verification is rigorous primarily after the semantic boundary has been fixed.**

And therefore:

> **Formal verification does not eliminate the interpretation problem; it begins after a substantial part of that problem has already been decided.**

The boundary is not a weakness of formal verification.

It is the condition that makes formal verification possible.

---

## 4.3 Then why do it?

This prevents the argument from becoming pessimistic.

Formal verification does **not** need to solve the entire regulatory problem to be valuable.

Its value lies precisely in making a **carefully defined part of the argument mathematically defensible**.

Rather than claiming:

> “Formal verification proves that the ADS obeys the law.”

a more defensible statement is:

> **“Given an explicit interpretation and a defined set of assumptions, formal verification can establish whether the system satisfies the resulting specification.”**

This is a narrower claim—but also a much stronger one.

The point is therefore not to reduce the role of formal methods.

It is to **locate their strongest leverage point**.

---

## 4.4 From context to interpretation: where the next frontier may lie

The most difficult transition in the entire chain may not be:

> specification → verification

but:

> **context → interpretation**

The real world produces an enormous, heterogeneous and continuously changing contextual state.

One possible future direction is therefore an intermediate **knowledge representation / knowledge base** that can structure this complexity:

> Real-world context
> ↓
> structured knowledge representation
> ↓
> interpretation model
> ↓
> formalizable core

Such a knowledge base could encode:

* entities and relationships
* road and environmental conditions
* traffic rules
* agent roles
* behavioral assumptions
* scenario semantics
* exceptions and dependencies
* relationships among different regulatory sources

### Could large language models play this role?

Potentially—but with an important qualification.

LLMs are exceptionally good at handling large volumes of heterogeneous language and extracting flexible contextual relationships from them. Their strength is therefore attractive precisely at the **context-to-interpretation interface**.

But their weakness is equally important:

> **The semantic structures they produce are not inherently reliable or formally grounded.**

An LLM may recognize that several pieces of context are related, describe their relationship, and construct an extremely rich and flexible representation.

But this does not necessarily mean that it **understands what that context normatively or physically entails**.

One way to characterize the problem:

> An LLM may provide an extraordinarily expressive **syntactic package of contextual interpretation**, without guaranteeing that the package corresponds to the intended semantics.

This suggests that the promising industrial direction is not simply:

> **LLM → formal specification**

but rather a mediated architecture involving:

* structured knowledge bases
* ontologies
* scenario representations
* retrieval / grounding
* domain-specific reasoning
* rule engines
* formal constraints
* human validation

The role of the LLM may therefore be less about becoming the final interpreter and more about **helping construct and navigate the knowledge representation from which interpretation can be made explicit and eventually formalized**.

This is also where the distinction established in Chapters 1–3 becomes practically meaningful:

> **The challenge is not merely making language machine-readable. It is making the semantics sufficiently explicit that mathematical reasoning becomes legitimate.**

# 5. Looking Beyond the Formalization Problem

**~300–400 words | ~10%**

This final section should **not provide a roadmap, solution, or prescription**.

Instead, after spending the article inside the struggle between regulation, interpretation and formal methods, the lens is raised one level.

When the transition from regulation to formal methods begins to feel increasingly difficult, perhaps the next step is not to keep pushing the mathematics harder.

Perhaps it is to **look up from the problem and examine the boundary itself**.

---

## 5.1 What is actually worth formalizing?

The original question:

> **Can we formalize regulations?**

is too coarse.

The more fundamental question is not even simply:

> **Which parts of a regulation should be formalized?**

but:

> **What latent structure inside a regulation needs to become explicit before formal reasoning becomes useful?**

A regulation contains more than explicit rules.

It may contain:

* normative boundaries;
* assumptions about context;
* implicit responsibilities;
* exceptions;
* relationships among agents;
* notions of acceptable behavior;
* thresholds that only become meaningful within a particular operational context.

Some of these can be made explicit.

Some can be formalized.

Some may remain dependent on interpretation and evidence.

And some may be better represented through testing, monitoring, or institutional judgment rather than forced into a formal specification.

The question therefore shifts from:

> *How do we formalize everything?*

to:

> **Where does formalization create the most defensible increase in rigor?**

---

## 5.2 Formal methods, used where they matter most

This does not diminish formal methods.

Quite the opposite.

Once the boundary is understood, formal methods can be applied to problems where their precision provides disproportionate value.

### 1. Refining performance boundaries

Formal methods can help connect:

> **scenario semantics → safety objectives → concrete verification / testing requirements**

For example, formalizing the relationship between scenario conditions, hazard classes and safety integrity assumptions can make a V&V process more **traceable**, rather than merely producing a large collection of test cases.

The value is not formalizing every scenario.

It is helping determine:

> **which scenarios matter, why they matter, and what evidence is sufficient.**

### 2. Formalizing the constraints that actually guide engineering attention

The most consequential constraints are not always the large, explicit regulatory statements visible to the public.

Engineering decisions are often shaped by smaller and more operationally important constraints:

* scenario-selection rules;
* assumptions;
* exclusion criteria;
* responsibility boundaries;
* system decomposition;
* evidence thresholds;
* conditions for escalating a case from simulation to physical testing.

These constraints determine:

> **what engineers focus on—and what they are allowed to ignore.**

They may attract little public attention and may not appear as headline regulatory requirements, yet they can become decisive in determining whether a V&V process is meaningful.

Formal methods can therefore be valuable not only for proving system properties, but for **making the reasoning behind engineering decisions explicit and traceable**.

### 3. Helping construct the knowledge base

Finally, formal methods may play a supporting role in the knowledge layer discussed above.

Rather than asking an LLM to directly generate a formal specification, formal methods can provide:

* schemas;
* ontologies;
* consistency constraints;
* typed relations;
* invariants;
* validation rules;
* machine-checkable structures.

This can constrain and ground the flexible but unreliable semantic representations produced by language models.

The resulting relationship is not:

> **LLM replaces formal methods**

but rather:

> **LLM expands the ability to handle contextual complexity; formal methods constrain and validate what can legitimately be inferred from it.**

The larger lesson is therefore not that one method should replace another.

It is that **different tools become powerful at different boundaries of the problem**.

And this is perhaps where we should leave the question.

> **“I suppose it is tempting, if the only tool you have is a hammer, to treat everything as if it were a nail.”**
> — Abraham Maslow, *The Psychology of Science: A Reconnaissance* (1966) ([维基引文][1])

The point is not to find one tool capable of formalizing the entire regulatory world.

It is to recognize **where each tool can make the argument stronger—and where another kind of reasoning is required.**
